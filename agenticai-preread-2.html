How to use this briefing

What should I expect, and what will I be able to do at the end?

This briefing is written for senior executives who do not need to implement the Model Context Protocol but do need to understand it well enough to make consequential decisions about it — about budgets, vendor selection, governance, and whether the AI initiatives the board is hearing about will actually scale.

You do not need a technical background. Where technical concepts appear, they are translated into the language of architecture, capital allocation, and operating model. Where the underlying detail matters for an executive decision, the briefing makes that explicit; where it does not, the briefing keeps moving.

Three ways to work through it

  • End-to-end (120 min): The recommended path. Read Modules 0–7 in order in a single sitting, ideally with a pen.
  • Two-session split (2×60 min): Modules 0–4 in the first session (the theory); Modules 5–7 in the second (the application). Best for working executives who want to discuss between sessions.
  • Briefing format (45 min): Read Modules 0, 1, 4, and 7 only. Use Module 5 as reference. This is the version to skim before a board meeting where MCP comes up.

What you will be able to do at the end

  1. Explain what MCP is, why it exists, and why it matters — in two minutes, without jargon.
  2. Recognise the architectural pattern in any vendor pitch, separating signal from marketing.
  3. Apply four decision frameworks to specific situations in your own organisation.
  4. Ask the four questions that distinguish a credible MCP strategy from a slide deck.
  5. Sponsor a 90-day pilot with a defensible governance posture.
A note on certainty

MCP is roughly eighteen months old as a public standard. It is already the default integration layer for enterprise AI agents, but the ecosystem around it — registries, gateways, security tooling, vendor commitments — is still hardening. This briefing distinguishes throughout between what is settled, what is emerging, and what is contested. Treat each accordingly.

[01.0] 15 minutes Module One

The strategic context — why this, why now

Before we know what MCP is, why should a senior leader pay attention?

1.1 The problem we keep solving badly

For thirty years, enterprises have been quietly paying a tax on integration. Every time a new system arrives — a CRM, an HR platform, a data warehouse, a contract repository, a fraud engine — engineering teams write custom connectors to the systems already in place. Every time a system is replaced, those connectors are rewritten. The middleware industry, the iPaaS market, the entire category of "integration platforms" exists to manage what is fundamentally a combinatorial problem: connecting M tools to N applications requires up to M × N bespoke integrations, and each of them needs to be maintained.

The arrival of large language models has not changed this dynamic. It has accelerated it. An LLM is useful in inverse proportion to how isolated it is from the data and tools the business actually runs on. The first wave of enterprise AI — chatbots that could only answer from a training-time snapshot of public knowledge — failed not because the models were weak, but because the models were unplugged. Every pilot that has stalled in the past eighteen months stalled at roughly the same point: the model is impressive in demo and useless in production, because production requires real data.

1.2 What changed

In November 2024, Anthropic published the Model Context Protocol — an open specification for how AI applications connect to external data and tools. It was a small announcement at the time. By the end of 2025, it had been adopted by OpenAI, Microsoft, Google DeepMind, AWS, and most of the agentic AI ecosystem. SDK downloads grew from roughly two million per month at launch to over ninety-seven million by Q1 2026 — a 50× increase in eighteen months. In December 2025, Anthropic transferred stewardship of the protocol to the Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation, co-founded with Block and OpenAI, signalling that MCP is no longer one company's project but shared infrastructure.

The historical parallel is HTTP. The protocol itself is unglamorous. What it unlocks — a marketplace of compatible components from a marketplace of suppliers — is transformative.

"It's a USB-C port for AI." The standard analogy — accurate for end-users, but conceals the strategic point: USB-C also reshaped the entire peripherals industry.

1.3 Why executives should care now, not later

The decision in front of senior leadership is not whether their organisation will adopt MCP. The market has settled that question. The decision is how, on what timeline, and with what posture. Three forces make the timing tight.

Procurement is shifting. Enterprise software vendors are now being asked, in RFPs, whether their products expose an MCP server. Forrester projects that thirty percent of enterprise application vendors will ship MCP servers in 2026. Salesforce, Atlassian, SAP, ServiceNow, and most major SaaS providers have committed publicly. Within twelve to eighteen months, lacking an MCP integration will be a procurement disqualifier in the same way that lacking SAML SSO became one in the late 2010s.

Internal AI investment is fragmenting. If your organisation has more than one team building AI applications — and almost every organisation of meaningful size does — those teams are currently writing custom connectors to internal systems. Each one is a future migration. The cost of harmonising them later, after each team has standardised on incompatible patterns, will exceed the cost of setting a standard now by an order of magnitude.

Governance is being defined in code. The earliest enterprise MCP deployments are establishing the patterns by which AI agents will be authorised, audited, and constrained. Organisations that engage now influence those patterns to suit their risk posture. Organisations that wait inherit them.

The bottom line

MCP is the integration layer for enterprise AI. It will be ubiquitous within two years. The strategic question for senior leadership is whether your organisation arrives at that point having shaped its adoption — or having been shaped by it.

[02.0] 20 minutes Module Two

MCP fundamentals — what it is, plainly

In one paragraph, what is the Model Context Protocol actually?

2.1 The definition

The Model Context Protocol is an open specification that defines a standard way for AI applications to discover and use external data sources and tools. It was introduced by Anthropic in November 2024 and is now governed by the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation. Technically, MCP rides on JSON-RPC 2.0 — a long-established message format — and supports two transport methods: a local mode where the AI application and the tool run on the same machine, and a remote mode over HTTPS for hosted services.

That is the technical description. The useful description is:

MCP is a contract that says: "Here is how an AI assistant asks for data, asks to do things, and finds out what it is allowed to do — in a way that does not depend on who built the AI or who built the tool."

A contract is the right metaphor. It does not do the work. It defines the shape of the work so that any compliant party can do their part and trust the other side to do theirs.

2.2 The problem MCP solves, stated formally

Before MCP, integrating LLMs with enterprise systems suffered from what engineers call the N × M problem:

  • N AI models or applications (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, internal agents, vertical agents) …
  • multiplied by M tools and data sources (Salesforce, SAP, Snowflake, internal databases, ticketing systems, document repositories) …
  • equals N × M custom integrations, each one a maintenance liability.

An enterprise with five AI applications and forty integrated systems is looking at potentially two hundred bespoke connectors. Each connector has its own authentication logic, its own error handling, its own version drift. Each is independently maintained by whoever wrote it. None are reusable across models.

MCP collapses this to N + M. Each tool implements one MCP server. Each AI application implements one MCP client. Any AI can connect to any tool. The economic shape of the problem changes.

Framework 01

The N × M to N + M Reduction

Without MCP: Five AI applications connecting to forty systems requires 5 × 40 = 200 custom integrations. Each must be authored, secured, and maintained per pairing.

With MCP: Five AI applications and forty systems require 5 + 40 = 45 standardised components — 40 MCP servers (one per system) and 5 MCP-capable clients (one per AI application). Any AI can use any tool.

Operational implication: The integration backlog stops growing with combinations and starts growing with new endpoints. The slope of integration cost flattens. This is the same shift that REST APIs created for inter-application integration in the 2000s, and it has the same long-tail consequences.

Figure 2.1 — The shape of the problem changes from multiplicative to additive

2.3 The USB-C analogy — useful and limited

The most widely-used analogy for MCP is "USB-C for AI." It is accurate in two ways and misleading in one.

It is accurate that before USB-C, every device had its own proprietary cable. After USB-C, one connector reaches any device. MCP plays the same role: one integration layer reaches any tool. It is also accurate that, like USB-C, the protocol is invisible to end-users — the value is the elimination of friction, not a new capability per se.

It is misleading because USB-C is principally a physical standard for power and data transfer. MCP is more like the combination of USB-C plus the device-discovery layer that lets your laptop know what kind of peripheral you just plugged in — a printer, a camera, a microphone — and present the right controls. MCP is not just connection; it is capability negotiation. An MCP-capable AI application can ask any MCP server, "What can you do? What permissions do you require? What inputs do you take?" and respond appropriately. That capability-discovery layer is where the strategic value lives.

2.4 What MCP is not

Three persistent confusions are worth heading off before they cause expensive misunderstandings in your organisation.

MCP is not an AI model. It does not replace, compete with, or supplement Claude, GPT, Gemini, or any other model. It is the connective tissue between models and tools. A poor model with great MCP integration is still a poor model.

MCP is not a replacement for APIs. Underneath an MCP server is almost always a normal API. MCP is a layer above APIs that AI agents understand natively. Your existing API investments are not threatened; they are wrapped.

MCP is not an agent framework. Frameworks like LangChain, LangGraph, or AutoGen orchestrate the decision-making logic of agents — what to think, when to act, how to chain steps. MCP is what those agents use to reach the outside world. They are complementary; many production deployments combine them.

2.5 Glossary — terms you will hear repeatedly

MCP Host
The AI application that the user interacts with. Examples: Claude Desktop, ChatGPT, Cursor, an internal AI agent your company builds. The Host coordinates Clients.
MCP Client
The component inside the Host that maintains a connection to a single MCP Server. A Host with five MCP integrations runs five Clients.
MCP Server
The component that exposes a tool or data source to MCP. Built once per tool by the vendor (e.g. Salesforce ships a Salesforce MCP Server) or by your engineering team for internal systems.
Tool
An action an AI can request — for example, "create a Jira ticket," "send an email," "run a SQL query." Tools must be invoked by the AI and typically require user approval at first use.
Resource
Read-only data the AI can pull into its context — a document, a file, a database snapshot, a calendar entry. Resources do not perform actions; they provide information.
Prompt
A reusable template that the server provides to the AI — for example, "summarise this case file in the format our legal team uses." Prompts let domain experts encode best practices.
Transport
How the Host and Server communicate. Two are defined: stdio (local, for tools running on the same machine) and HTTP (remote, for hosted services).
Capability negotiation
The handshake at the start of each connection where Client and Server tell each other which features each supports. This is what makes MCP graceful across versions.
Sampling
An advanced feature that allows a Server to ask the AI model to perform a task — for example, summarising data the Server retrieved. It inverts the usual direction of the conversation.
[03.0] 20 minutes Module Three

Architecture & primitives

What is actually happening when an AI uses MCP — and which of those moving parts matters to me?

3.1 The three roles

Every MCP interaction involves three components. Their separation is the source of MCP's flexibility, so understanding them — at least conceptually — is worth the five minutes.

Figure 3.1 — The Host / Client / Server architecture, simplified

The Host is the AI application the user actually interacts with. It runs the model, manages the conversation, and presents results. When a user opens Claude Desktop and asks a question, Claude Desktop is the Host. The Host is also the security boundary — it decides which tools are available, asks the user for permission to use them, and enforces policy.

The Client is a connection manager that lives inside the Host. There is one Client per connected Server. If your Host is connected to Salesforce, Jira, and an internal database, the Host is running three Clients. The Client is internal plumbing; senior leadership rarely needs to think about it.

The Server is the component that exposes a tool or data source. It is the only piece you procure or build. Vendors ship Servers for their products; internal teams build Servers for internal systems. The Server is also where business logic and access control live.

Why the separation matters: any compliant Host can connect to any compliant Server. If you replace Claude with a different model tomorrow, your existing Servers still work. If a vendor ships an MCP Server for their product, every MCP-capable AI in your organisation can use it. This is why MCP reduces vendor lock-in — not because it is universally compatible by accident, but because the architecture deliberately separates the model from the integration.

3.2 The three primitives

An MCP Server exposes exactly three categories of capability. Together they cover the surface area of what AI agents need to do.

Framework 02

The Three Primitives

Resources — read-only data. A Resource is anything the AI can read but not change: a document, a database snapshot, a file in a repository. Resources are how the AI gets context.

Tools — actions with side effects. A Tool is anything the AI can do: create a record, send a message, query a database, schedule a meeting. Tools change the world. They typically require user authorisation, especially on first use.

Prompts — reusable templates. A Prompt is a template the Server offers to the Host — for example, "draft a customer email in our brand voice." Prompts let domain experts ship the right way to use a system, not just the raw ability.

Why three? The split maps to a real distinction in risk. Resources need data governance. Tools need authorisation and audit. Prompts need version control and ownership. Treating them differently in your governance is correct precisely because they are different categories.

Figure 3.2 — The three primitives, separated by who invokes them and who governs them

3.3 The transport layer

MCP runs over JSON-RPC 2.0 — a long-established, well-understood message format. It does not invent new networking. There are two transports defined.

stdio (standard input/output) is used when the Host and Server are running on the same machine. The Host launches the Server as a subprocess and talks to it through pipes. This is the most common pattern for early MCP adoption: an executive opens Claude Desktop, installs an MCP Server for, say, their local file system, and Claude Desktop runs that Server in the background. The data never leaves the machine. Latency is negligible.

HTTP (with Server-Sent Events for streaming) is used for remote Servers — hosted services that multiple Hosts connect to over the network. This is the pattern for enterprise deployments: a centrally-operated MCP Server fronts your CRM, and every AI application in your organisation connects to it over HTTPS, authenticated and audited centrally.

For senior leadership, the relevant distinction is operational: stdio servers are simple and isolated; HTTP servers are scalable but require the same security posture as any other internet-exposed service. Most early production deployments use HTTP servers behind a gateway that handles authentication, rate limiting, audit logging, and tool-level access controls.

Figure 3.3 — Two transports, two operating models

3.4 Capability negotiation — the part that future-proofs you

When a Host connects to a Server, the first exchange is not a tool call. It is a handshake. Each side declares which features of the MCP specification it supports. This negotiation is why MCP can evolve without breaking deployments.

Figure 3.4 — The three-phase exchange between any compliant Client and Server

The strategic point is that you do not have to wait for everyone to be on the same version. A Server that supports a newer feature will degrade gracefully when talking to an older Client. A Client that supports advanced features will use them when the Server can and fall back when it cannot. This is how the protocol stays usable through evolution — and why the standard is harder to fragment than it might appear.

3.5 Where this is heading — code execution and agents

In late 2025 and into 2026, a refinement has been emerging: rather than describing every tool to the AI model up-front (which consumes large amounts of the model's "context window" and slows it down), AI agents increasingly use MCP servers by writing code that calls them, the same way a human developer would call an API. Anthropic's published benchmarks show this approach reducing token usage by roughly 98% in agent workflows that span many tools.

The reason this matters at the executive level is not the engineering detail but the trajectory: MCP is becoming the substrate for AI agents that operate at machine speed and scale, not the interface for one-off chatbot queries. The cost and performance characteristics of an MCP-based architecture are improving steadily, which removes one of the early objections to broad enterprise adoption.

A reality check

MCP is a young protocol. The specification has changed materially in its first eighteen months and will continue to evolve. Production deployments need a deliberate strategy for tracking specification updates, qualifying new versions, and managing version skew across internal Servers and external Hosts. Treat this as the cost of being early.

[04.0] 15 minutes Module Four

Executive frameworks

How do I make defensible decisions about MCP in my own organisation?

This module presents four frameworks for executive decision-making. They are not exhaustive, but together they cover the major decisions a senior leader will face in the next eighteen months.

4.1 The Build vs. Buy vs. Standardise decision

For every system that AI agents need to reach, there are three options, and the wrong choice is expensive.

Framework 03

The Build vs. Buy vs. Standardise Matrix

Option When it fits Watch out for
Buy
(vendor's MCP server)		 Vendor-provided systems with stable APIs and active vendor MCP support (Salesforce, GitHub, Slack, Atlassian). Vendor lock-in on the connector itself; lag time on new vendor features; access control granularity may not match your policies.
Build
(internal MCP server)		 Proprietary internal systems, systems with sensitive data that must not traverse third-party infrastructure, or workflows requiring deep customisation. Maintenance burden — typically 20–30% of original build cost annually; staffing the role of "MCP server owner" through the lifecycle.
Standardise
(managed MCP gateway)		 Mid-sized organisations connecting many systems quickly, or organisations that want centralised governance, rate limiting, and audit without bespoke engineering per server. Adds a vendor to the critical path; gateway-level outages affect every connected AI; pricing models still maturing.

The discipline: Apply this framework system-by-system, not enterprise-wide. Most mature deployments end up with a mix — buy for major SaaS, build for differentiated internal systems, standardise via a gateway for long-tail integrations.

4.2 The value driver framework

MCP creates value in five distinct ways. Pilots that succeed are clear about which driver they are pursuing. Pilots that fail tend to chase all five at once, which makes the business case unmeasurable.

Framework 04

The Five MCP Value Drivers

1. Integration cost reduction. Replacing N × M custom integrations with N + M standardised ones. The savings are largest in organisations with many AI applications or many systems. Measurable in engineering hours saved per integration.

2. Time-to-deployment for new AI applications. Once your Servers exist, a new AI application can reach them in days, not months. The measurable signal is the calendar time between "we want an AI assistant for X" and "it is in production."

3. Vendor flexibility and model portability. When the integration layer is standard, switching the underlying AI model — from Claude to GPT, from a hosted model to an on-premises one — does not require rewiring. This is option value, hard to quantify but real.

4. Governance and audit maturity. A standardised integration layer is a single point where access control, rate limiting, logging, and compliance reporting can be enforced. Compliance teams will thank you; auditors more so.

5. Compounding agent capability. As more systems are exposed as MCP Servers, each new agent can do more, immediately, without bespoke integration. The value of the Nth Server is highest when the prior N-1 already exist. This is a network effect inside your own organisation.

4.3 The MCP maturity model

Organisations adopting MCP pass through roughly five stages. Knowing where you are is more useful than chasing where you should be — the next stage is almost always the right target.

0

Unaware or exploratory

No production MCP deployments. Engineers may be experimenting with stdio servers locally. No central inventory, no governance posture. Most organisations are here as of early 2026.

1

First production server

One MCP Server in production, typically connecting a single AI application to a single internal system. Often a developer-productivity workflow (code review, documentation search). The ROI of this stage is local but visible.

2

Departmental adoption

Multiple Servers serving one business function or department — typically engineering, customer support, or sales operations. A pattern emerges; reusable components appear. Governance starts to formalise.

3

Enterprise gateway

A central MCP gateway is in place. All AI applications connect through it. Authentication, rate limiting, audit, and tool-level access controls are managed centrally. New Servers are onboarded through a documented process.

4

Agent-native operating model

MCP is assumed by every new system as a standard interface. Business processes are designed with AI agent participation in mind. The organisation operates at hybrid human-agent throughput. Very few organisations are at this stage as of 2026; the leaders are pulling ahead.

4.4 The four questions to ask any vendor

Senior leaders are increasingly being pitched MCP-related products and services. The pitches are getting better; the underlying offerings vary wildly. The following four questions separate substance from positioning.

Framework 05

The Vendor Diligence Four

1. Which version of the specification do you support, and what is your policy for tracking updates? The spec is still evolving. A vendor that cannot answer crisply is one whose roadmap you will be debugging.

2. How do you handle tool authorisation and audit? An MCP Server that exposes destructive actions (delete, transfer, send) without per-user identity propagation and immutable audit logs is a compliance incident waiting to happen.

3. What is your posture on tool description trust? Tool descriptions arrive over the wire and are read by the AI. A malicious server could change them after approval — the "rug pull" risk. Ask how the vendor mitigates this.

4. What happens to my data? Does the vendor's MCP infrastructure see the queries, the responses, both, or neither? Where does the data physically reside? Get this in writing.

[05.0] 30 minutes Module Five

Four business use cases, four different organisations

What does MCP adoption actually look like in practice — and what should each context teach me?

The four cases below are composites — synthesised from publicly-reported deployments and engagement patterns observed across early adopters in each sector. They are not specific to a single company, but every detail is drawn from real production patterns. Each case is structured identically so that you can compare across them. The goal is not the specific success — it is the shape of how MCP creates value differently in different contexts.

[05.1] Case Study One

The analyst workbench

Financial Services
Organisation profile
Tier 1 global investment bank. Roughly 60,000 employees across 30 countries. Three principal lines of business — investment banking advisory (M&A, capital markets), global markets (sales and trading), and asset management. Highly matrixed organisation with a deeply siloed data estate: roughly 4,200 internal applications, a mix of vendor-sourced and proprietary, accreted over four decades of M&A and platform consolidation.
Business problem
A junior analyst preparing a pitchbook for an M&A deal team spends, on average, sixty to seventy percent of their time pulling data — comparable transactions from one system, target financials from a second, ownership structures from a third, recent precedent deals from a fourth, regulatory filings from a fifth. The data is all available; the friction is access. Senior bankers are paying analyst hours for what is fundamentally a retrieval problem. Earlier attempts at a unified "analyst portal" were abandoned because every new data source required a custom integration, and the maintenance burden grew faster than the team could absorb.
MCP application
The bank stood up MCP Servers for fifteen of its highest-traffic internal systems — the deal database, the financials warehouse, the precedent transaction library, the comparable transactions store, regulatory filings, and several research repositories. Each Server enforces row-level access control tied to the analyst's existing entitlements, so the AI never reaches data the analyst could not access directly. The Servers sit behind an internal MCP gateway that handles authentication via the bank's existing SSO, audit logging into the bank's SIEM, and tool-level rate limiting.
  • Host: a custom internal AI workbench built on Claude, deployed in the bank's secure cloud tenancy.
  • Servers built: 15 (initial wave), all internal. No third-party MCP servers are connected to production analyst workflows.
  • Governance posture: every Tool call is logged, attributed to a named user, and reviewable by compliance.
Outcomes
~55%
Reduction in pitchbook prep time
3×
Increase in coverage breadth per analyst
~40%
Reduction in data-related errors

The headline number — pitchbook time — was less important to the executive sponsor than the second one. Analysts were now covering significantly more potential clients with the same headcount, which translated directly into top-of-funnel growth for the advisory business. The error reduction came as a surprise; the AI's structured retrieval was more consistent than analysts working under time pressure.

Executive lessons
  • The value is in the second-order metric. The headline efficiency gain was real, but the strategic case was reinvested capacity, not cost savings.
  • Access control is the unlock. The decisive design choice was binding every Server to existing user entitlements. Without that, compliance would have killed the project; with it, the project moved through risk review in six weeks.
  • Build, don't buy, when the systems are proprietary. No vendor was going to ship an MCP Server for an internal deal database. The bank invested in MCP engineering capability — five people, dedicated — and treated it as platform infrastructure.
[05.2] Case Study Two

Care coordination at the bedside

Healthcare
Organisation profile
Integrated academic medical centre. A multi-hospital health system anchored by an academic teaching hospital and affiliated with a university medical school. Roughly 35,000 employees, including 3,500 attending physicians and 1,500 residents. Operates on a single instance of a major EHR (electronic health record) system, plus dozens of specialty systems for imaging, laboratory, pharmacy, scheduling, and clinical research. Subject to HIPAA, state-level privacy regimes, and research ethics oversight.
Business problem
Complex inpatients — particularly older patients with multiple comorbidities — generate enormous quantities of relevant clinical context that is distributed across the EHR, imaging archives, prior outpatient encounters, medication histories, and recent laboratory results. The attending physician's task on rounds is to absorb all of this in the few minutes before seeing the patient. Documentation burden — the time physicians spend at the keyboard rather than at the bedside — has been identified as a primary driver of clinician burnout.
MCP application
The health system deployed an in-room AI assistant integrated with MCP Servers for the EHR, the imaging archive, and the pharmacy system. Resources expose read-only access to the patient's chart; Tools allow the assistant to draft (but not finalise) progress notes, order sets, and discharge instructions. The system is voice-activated and runs entirely within the health system's cloud — no patient data crosses the boundary.
  • Host: an EHR-integrated assistant from a clinical AI vendor.
  • Servers used: 8 — three vendor-supplied (EHR, imaging, pharmacy), five internally built for adjacent systems (labs, research databases, scheduling, social work intake, billing).
  • Governance posture: every Resource read is logged against the patient's chart; every Tool draft requires physician sign-off before it becomes part of the record; an IRB-equivalent review approved the deployment pattern.
Outcomes
~90 min
Daily documentation time saved per attending
~25%
Reduction in clinician burnout score
~15%
Improvement in discharge-summary completeness

Burnout reduction, measured via a standardised instrument over twelve weeks, was the outcome that secured continued executive sponsorship. Physician retention is the binding constraint for any teaching hospital; an intervention that demonstrably improves it is treated as a strategic investment, not an IT project.

Executive lessons
  • Draft, do not commit. The architectural choice that made this acceptable to clinicians was the strict line between AI-drafted artefacts and physician-signed ones. Tools never finalise anything that touches the patient record.
  • Compliance is a feature, not a tax. The audit trail produced by MCP Tool calls turned out to be more comprehensive than the prior manual logging — and was treated as a compliance upgrade by the privacy office.
  • Mixed sourcing is normal. Some Servers were vendor-built, some internal. The health system did not insist on uniformity; the MCP gateway absorbed the heterogeneity.
[05.3] Case Study Three

The unified service agent

Retail & E-commerce
Organisation profile
Mid-market omnichannel retailer. Annual revenue in the $2–4B range. Roughly 350 physical stores, a substantial e-commerce business, and a private-label credit card. Specialty apparel and home goods. Operates a relatively modern but stitched-together stack: a vendor commerce platform, a separate POS system, a CDP (customer data platform), a third-party logistics provider for fulfilment, and a customer service platform with ~600 agents handling roughly 40,000 contacts per week.
Business problem
Average handle time on customer service calls is dominated by "swivel-chair" work — the agent toggling between four or five systems to find the customer's order, their loyalty status, the shipment's location, and the relevant policy. Customer satisfaction is highest when the agent has full context; it collapses when the customer has to repeat themselves to the agent or the agent has to put them on hold to look something up. An earlier attempt at an agent-desktop dashboard failed when one of the underlying vendors changed their API and the unified view broke; the retailer was unwilling to assume the maintenance burden of bespoke integrations again.
MCP application
The retailer connected its customer service platform's AI assistant — already in use for ticket routing — to MCP Servers for the commerce platform (vendor-supplied), the CDP (vendor-supplied), and the 3PL's tracking system (vendor-supplied), plus an internal MCP Server for the loyalty database. The assistant retrieves the full customer context the moment a call connects and surfaces it for the agent. It can also be asked to perform routine actions — issuing a refund within policy limits, sending a replacement, or scheduling a callback.
  • Host: the customer service platform's native AI agent layer.
  • Servers used: 4 (3 vendor-supplied, 1 internal). The retailer's engineering investment was deliberately small.
  • Governance posture: Tools are scoped to actions within written customer service policy; anything outside policy escalates to a supervisor. Every Tool call is logged.

The first-contact resolution improvement matters most operationally: every contact that does not require a callback removes load from the call centre. CSAT is the visible signal to the executive committee, but FCR is what funds the next investment.

Executive lessons
  • Buy when the vendor is ready. Three of the four Servers were vendor-supplied. The retailer's small engineering team would not have built four Servers from scratch in a reasonable timeline; the existence of vendor MCP support was the unlock.
  • Bound the Tools by policy. The AI was authorised to take actions only within explicit customer-service policy. This was a deliberate choice to avoid AI judgement on edge cases; edge cases route to humans.
  • Watch vendor MCP roadmaps closely. The architectural decision that paid off was choosing platforms with credible vendor commitments to MCP. The retailer's CIO now treats "MCP roadmap" as a standard procurement question.
[05.4] Case Study Four

The field service intelligence layer

Industrial Manufacturing
Organisation profile
Industrial equipment OEM. Designs, manufactures, and services heavy industrial equipment — turbines, compressors, large-scale rotating machinery — installed in customer plants worldwide. Roughly 80,000 employees, with ~12,000 field service engineers operating out of regional service centres. The installed base spans decades of equipment generations, each with its own documentation, telemetry pattern, and service procedures. After-market service contracts are a substantial share of profit.
Business problem
When equipment in the field reports an anomaly, the field service engineer dispatched to investigate must synthesise data from at least four distinct sources: the equipment's own telemetry stream, the service history for that specific unit, the engineering documentation for that generation of equipment, and any open quality issues affecting similar units. Senior engineers carry this synthesis in their heads. Junior engineers spend hours in pre-dispatch preparation and still arrive on-site under-informed. Time-to-diagnosis directly determines uptime for the customer and margin for the OEM.
MCP application
The OEM built MCP Servers for the four sources its engineers most needed: the IIoT telemetry platform, the service history database, the engineering documentation repository, and the open-issue tracking system. The Servers feed an AI assistant that field engineers can query before and during a service call from a ruggedised tablet. The assistant produces a structured pre-dispatch briefing: likely causes, recommended diagnostics, parts likely to be needed, and similar cases recently resolved.
  • Host: a custom internal field-service application embedding an AI model.
  • Servers built: 4 internal, plus 2 vendor-supplied (one for the IIoT platform, one for the engineering documentation system).
  • Governance posture: The assistant is advisory; it does not order parts or commit to service actions autonomously. All recommendations are logged for post-event learning.
Outcomes
~30%
Reduction in mean-time-to-diagnosis
~22%
Reduction in repeat visits
+12%
Field engineer NPS

The repeat-visit reduction is the financial headline — each repeat visit costs the OEM roughly $4,000 in dispatched labour and parts logistics, before considering the relationship damage of repeated outages. The engineer NPS lift was unanticipated: experienced engineers had assumed AI tooling would devalue their expertise; in practice, the assistant freed them from clerical retrieval, which they experienced as respect.

Executive lessons
  • Decades of accreted systems are the use case. MCP earns its keep most clearly where the underlying data is heterogeneous, distributed, and slow-changing — exactly the profile of an industrial installed base.
  • Advisory before autonomous. The OEM deliberately kept the AI in an advisory role for the first eighteen months. The autonomous version is on the roadmap, but only after a body of supervised decisions has been accumulated.
  • The workforce response is not what you expect. The risk of demoralising experienced engineers was managed not by message but by design — the assistant amplifies their judgement rather than replacing it. The NPS improvement was the proof.

5.5 Reading across the four cases

Four organisations, four sectors, four very different starting points. Common patterns are worth naming explicitly.

The systems are old; the integration layer is new. None of the four cases involved replacing the underlying systems. The investment was in wrapping them. This is the financial logic that makes MCP fundable: it preserves prior IT investment rather than competing with it.

Mixed sourcing is the norm, not an anomaly. Every case combined vendor-supplied and internally built Servers. Insisting on uniformity would have slowed each project; allowing heterogeneity, behind a consistent governance layer, made them feasible.

Tools are scoped before they are powerful. In every case, the AI's authority to take action was deliberately bounded — to policy in retail, to drafts in healthcare, to advisory in manufacturing. Autonomy was earned over time, not granted up front.

The second-order metric is the strategic case. Headline efficiency numbers got the projects funded; the executive sponsors actually cared about coverage breadth, clinician retention, first-contact resolution, repeat-visit rates, and engineer engagement. These are the outcomes that translate into strategic capacity.

[06.0] 10 minutes Module Six

Governance, risk & the 90-day plan

What can go wrong, and what should I do in the next quarter?

6.1 The risk surface, plainly

MCP introduces meaningful new risk surface. The risks are manageable; they are not zero. Senior leadership should understand them well enough not to be surprised when security or audit raises them.

Figure 6.1 — The four risk vectors and where each one crosses your trust boundary
Prompt injection through tool inputs

An MCP Tool's inputs come from the AI, which in turn is influenced by everything in its context — including content from other Tools and Resources. A malicious document or webpage in the AI's context can attempt to coerce the AI into calling a Tool destructively. The mitigation is treating every Tool input as untrusted and validating it at the Server boundary.

Tool description trust ("rug pull")

An MCP Server tells the Host what its Tools do via descriptions that the AI reads. A compromised Server could change a Tool's description after the user has approved it — for example, "send email" could quietly become "exfiltrate data." The mitigation is pinning approved Tool definitions and re-prompting users when descriptions change materially.

Cross-tool data leakage

An AI agent connected to multiple Servers can shuttle data between them in ways no individual Server can see. Sensitive data from a CRM Server can flow into a public-web Server. The mitigation is per-Tool data classification and Host-level enforcement of cross-Tool data flow policies.

Identity propagation gaps

If the user's identity does not propagate through the Host, through the Client, to the Server, then the Server cannot enforce row-level access control and the audit trail loses attribution. The mitigation is end-to-end identity propagation — implemented in every successful deployment in Module 5.

6.2 The governance framework

A defensible MCP governance posture has six elements. None of them are exotic; they are the elements any enterprise integration platform would have, adapted to the AI context.

Framework 06

The MCP Governance Hexagon

  1. Server inventory and ownership. Every Server has a named owner, a documented purpose, and a maintenance commitment. No anonymous Servers in production.
  2. Identity propagation end-to-end. Every Tool call is attributed to a named human user. No service accounts in primary paths.
  3. Tool authorisation policy. A documented policy specifies which Tools are available to which users in which contexts. Approval workflows for new Tools.
  4. Audit and observability. Every Tool call, every Resource read, with payloads where compliance requires, into the existing SIEM and analytics platforms. Treated as a first-class log source.
  5. Rate limiting and circuit breakers. AI agents can call Tools at machine speed. Rate limits prevent both abuse and accidental cost runs.
  6. Version and specification tracking. A named owner for tracking spec updates, qualifying new versions, and managing rollouts. Treated like any other dependency.

6.3 The 90-day executive action plan

Most senior leaders reading this briefing will conclude that they should do something about MCP. The right something is small, deliberate, and visible. The following is a defensible 90-day plan that does not require a new strategy document, a new function, or a new budget line — only sponsorship.

1

Days 1–30 — Inventory and orient

Commission a one-page inventory of MCP-related activity already underway in your organisation. Almost every organisation has at least three: an engineer experimenting locally, a vendor pitch under evaluation, and a procurement question waiting for an answer. Identify the named individual best positioned to convene the others.

2

Days 31–60 — Pilot a single, bounded use case

Choose one workflow with a clear executive sponsor, a measurable outcome, and a tightly bounded scope — ideally one AI application, one to three MCP Servers, one team. The pilot's primary deliverable is not the workflow itself; it is the governance pattern you intend to scale. The workflow should be in production by day 60.

3

Days 61–90 — Codify and propose

By the end of the quarter, the inventory has become a register, the pilot has produced governance artefacts, and the named convenor has a proposal for a twelve-month roadmap. Present at the next operating committee. Decide whether to fund a platform investment or remain in deliberate experimentation. Either decision is defensible; what is not defensible is making the decision implicitly.

6.4 Talking points for the board

Should MCP come up in board discussion — and within the next twelve months it will — the following four points are calibrated for that audience. They are not slides; they are language.

  • "MCP is the integration layer for enterprise AI agents — the equivalent of REST for AI, and the market has settled on it." States the strategic conclusion without engineering detail.
  • "Our exposure is twofold: vendors will increasingly require it, and our internal AI investments are fragmenting without it." Names both the procurement and the internal cost.
  • "We have a 90-day plan to inventory, pilot, and decide. The pilot is bounded; the decision is what we fund in the following year." Demonstrates considered action, not commitment to an unbounded programme.
  • "The right governance posture is established now, when patterns are still flexible. Waiting to formalise governance is more expensive than establishing it early." Pre-empts the "shouldn't we wait?" objection.
[07.0] 5 minutes Module Seven

Synthesis & discussion

What is the single thing I should remember from this briefing?

The four cases in Module 5 are different organisations doing different things in different sectors with different cost structures. What they share is harder to see and worth naming: each of them made an explicit choice to treat the integration layer for AI as infrastructure rather than as a project deliverable. That choice did not look glamorous when it was made. Looking back, it is the choice that compounded.

If there is one thing to remember from this briefing, that is it. The model gets the headlines. The integration layer determines whether the model does anything useful, whether you can replace it without rewiring, whether you can audit it, and whether the next AI application your organisation deploys takes six days or six months to reach production.

The seven points to carry forward

Synthesis

  1. MCP is the standard integration layer for enterprise AI. The question for senior leadership is no longer whether to engage but how.
  2. The architectural trick is the separation of Host, Client, and Server. Any AI reaches any tool. This is what reduces vendor lock-in and enables compounding.
  3. The three primitives — Resources, Tools, Prompts — map to different categories of risk and require different governance.
  4. The five value drivers are integration cost, time-to-deployment, vendor flexibility, governance maturity, and compounding agent capability. The fifth becomes dominant over time.
  5. Build, buy, and standardise are all valid choices. Apply the matrix system-by-system; the right mix is heterogeneous.
  6. Real production deployments share four patterns: integration-layer-as-platform, mixed sourcing, scoped tool authority, and second-order metrics as the strategic case.
  7. The 90-day plan is inventory, pilot, codify. The decision at day 90 is what you fund — but the decision must be made deliberately.

7.1 Discussion questions for an executive group

For your leadership team
  1. Three years from now, will our organisation have one integration layer for AI applications, or many? What does each version cost?
  2. If a major vendor announced an MCP Server next week, what is the current process for evaluating whether we connect to it? Is that process adequate for the rate at which vendors are now announcing them?
  3. For each of the five value drivers, which two are most strategic for us in the next twelve months? Which two are merely nice-to-have?
  4. What would the first pilot look like for us? Who would sponsor it? What governance pattern would it produce that we would want to scale?
  5. Where in our organisation is the right home for the named convenor of this work? IT? CTO office? The CDO function? A line of business?
  6. What is the most defensible reason for us to not move in the next quarter? Does that reason hold up to scrutiny?
