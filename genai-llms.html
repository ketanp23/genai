01
Vocabulary, minus the jargon

What we are actually talking about

Four words get used as if they mean the same thing. They don't, and in a client room the difference matters. Artificial intelligence is the broad field. Machine learning is the part of it where a system learns patterns from examples instead of following rules someone typed. Generative AI is the slice of machine learning that produces new content — text, images, audio, code. A large language model is the specific engine underneath most of the text tools you have used.

The four, side by side what actually differs

These are four stages of one lineage, not four rival technologies. The line that matters commercially sits between the first two columns: classical machine learning needs a human to decide which variables count, and deep learning does not — a neural network works out its own features straight from raw pixels, audio or text. That single difference is why computer vision, speech and eventually language became possible at all, and it is worth being able to say out loud in a client room.

Vocabulary and arithmetic

The words, and what they cost

Six terms carry most of the confusion in this field, and two numbers — tokens in and tokens out — carry most of the cost. Both are worth getting straight before anything else, because every later argument about reliability, price and feasibility is really an argument about one of them.

A token is a chunk of text — often a whole short word, sometimes part of a long one. The model reads and writes in tokens, not letters. English runs at roughly 1.3 tokens per word, so a 40-page report is about 25,000 tokens.

The five words that explain most behaviour
  • Parameters The billions of internal dials set during training, holding patterns in compressed form. Not a database you can look up or edit.
    In practice: you cannot "go in and fix" the fact that it thinks your client is headquartered in Mumbai. You correct it in every prompt, or you connect it to a source that says otherwise.
  • Context window How much text it can hold in view at once — your question, your pasted documents, the conversation so far. Everything outside it does not exist.
    In practice: an hour into a long chat about the Sanand plant it starts getting the throughput numbers wrong. Nothing broke — those tokens fell out of the window.
  • Temperature How boldly it picks among likely next words. Low is consistent and dull, high is inventive and unreliable.
    In practice: extracting renewal dates from 40 contracts wants the lowest setting available. Generating 40 campaign territories wants the highest.
  • Knowledge cutoff The date the training text stops. Anything after it is unknown, or worse, confidently guessed.
    In practice: ask about a Union Budget announcement from last month and a tool without search will produce plausible allocations that were never made.
  • Hallucination Fluent output that is simply not true — a direct consequence of optimising for plausible text, not a bug awaiting a patch.
    In practice: "as required under Section 7(3) of the Act" — correct-sounding legislation, correct-sounding subsection, no such provision.

Tokens are counted twice, and the two counts behave nothing like each other. Input tokens are everything that travels up: the hidden house instructions, any document you attached, the conversation so far, and your actual message. Output tokens are what the model writes back. Input arrives in bulk and is cheap. Output trickles out one token at a time, costs several times more each, and is the reason you sit watching an answer appear.

Token economics — what you are actually paying for live · illustrative prices

Two numbers sit on every bill, and they are not priced the same. Input tokens are everything you send — the instructions, the pasted report, the whole conversation so far. Output tokens are what it writes back. Output typically costs several times more per token than input, because generating is the expensive part, and because each output token has to be produced one at a time.

  • The conversation re-sends itself. There is no memory between turns, so turn five sends turns one to four again. A long chat over a big document is not five cheap calls; it is five increasingly expensive ones.
  • Reading is cheap, writing is dear. Summarising a 40-page report costs a lot of input and very little output. "Draft me a 30-page report" reverses that and costs far more.
  • Output length also drives waiting time. Latency tracks output tokens almost linearly, which is why asking for "five bullets" feels instant and "a full document" does not.
  • Caching changes the maths. Most providers now discount repeated input, so pasting the same document across many questions is cheaper than the raw sum suggests — if the tool supports it.
  • The real comparison is labour. Judge the bill against the hourly cost of the person who would otherwise have done it, not against zero.

05
The exclusive question

What it can actually do

"Can AI do this?" is the wrong question, because the answer is almost always a heavily qualified yes. The useful question is which family of ability a task belongs to — because reliability, the amount of checking required and the value you get are properties of the family, not of the tool. There are about a dozen families worth knowing, and every consulting use case you will meet is a combination of them.

The capability map colour shows how much checking each needs
blue · strong, and cheap to check amber · strong, but must be verified pink · weak or unverifiable — handle with care
Capability explorer click one for the example and a prompt to steal
Where the value concentrates in an engagement
  • Anything unstructured becoming structured. Transcripts to themes, emails to a tracker, a stack of PDFs to a comparison table. This is the single biggest source of time saved in consulting work, and it is also the safest, because the source is in front of it.
  • Anything that exists in one form and is needed in another. The same finding for a plant manager, a chief financial officer and a regulator; English into Gujarati; a memo into a slide skeleton.
  • Anything where volume beats precision. Forty hypotheses, thirty names, twenty questions for tomorrow's expert call. You are buying range, and you were always going to select from it.
  • Anything you can check by running it. Code, formulas, queries. The computer tells you whether it worked, which makes this far safer than its reputation suggests.
  • Anything that benefits from a tireless critic. "Argue against this recommendation" costs thirty seconds and regularly surfaces the objection the client actually raises.
Where it genuinely cannot help
  • Facts about your client that nobody wrote down. No amount of prompting reaches what was said in the corridor.
  • Being accountable. It cannot sign, cannot be liable, and cannot be in the meeting when the number is challenged.
  • Knowing what matters. It weights the trivial finding and the killer finding about the same unless you tell it which is which — and telling it requires you to already know.
  • Reliable arithmetic on your figures. It will often be right, and it will write the same confident sentence when it is not.
  • Telling you when it is out of its depth. There is no internal signal separating recall from reconstruction, so there is nothing honest for it to report.
  • Original primary research. It cannot run the survey, visit the store, or get the plant manager to say the thing they only say on the third visit.
The one-line test for any new use case. Ask: is the answer sitting in something I can hand it, or does it have to come out of the model's memory? Then ask: if it is wrong, who finds out, and when? Two sentences, and you have placed the task on the capability map and in the risk grid at the same time.
06
A usable decision rule

Where it is reliable, where it is risky

Two questions settle almost every case. Can you check the output cheaply? And what happens if it is wrong and nobody catches it? Plot a task on those two axes and the right way to use the tool falls out of the grid.

How to read the grid what it is for, and what it is not

What it is for

It is a triage device, not a truth. Its job is to convert an argument that usually goes "should we be using AI for this?" — which nobody can settle — into two narrower questions that a team can actually answer in thirty seconds each. The output is not a yes or a no. It is a working method: who drafts, who checks, and how hard.

Reading the horizontal axis — how cheaply can you check it?

Not "is it checkable in principle", but what checking actually costs you: time, access and expertise. If the source is in the room and any analyst can verify it in ten minutes, the task sits far right. If verifying means commissioning primary research, calling three experts, or being the one person in the firm who knows the regulation, it sits far left.

Reading the vertical axis — what does a miss cost?

Ask who is harmed, how visibly, and how late it would surface. An awkward sentence in an internal note is cheap. A wrong number in the investment case is expensive, and expensive twice over because it surfaces in front of the client. Reputational and regulatory exposure count here, not just money.

How to use it in a team

  • Place the task, not the tool. The same tool moves quadrants depending on the job.
  • Argue about the position. The disagreement is the value — it usually surfaces that two people had different ideas of who would check the work.
  • Write the method next to the dot. "Analyst drafts, manager verifies against source, partner signs" is the actual output.
  • Re-place it when things change. New tool, new data access or a new client sensitivity moves the dot. This is a live map, not a policy.
The most common mistake. Assuming the top-left quadrant means "AI is useless here". It means do not delegate the answer. Using it to attack your own reasoning, list what you might have missed, or rehearse the client's objections is still valuable in that quadrant — you simply never let its output travel unedited.

Know the shapes

How it fails, and what that looks like

Failures have recognisable signatures. Learn the three below — an invented source, a wrong number in a confident sentence, and the finding that quietly went missing — and you will catch most of them in a skim rather than in a client meeting.

The confidentiality problem, separately
  • What you paste, you have disclosed. Client data in a personal consumer account may be stored, reviewed, or used to improve the service, depending on the plan and settings. Under most engagement letters and NDAs that alone is a breach, whether or not anything leaks.
  • The rule to internalise. Firm-approved tool with the enterprise agreement in place, or nothing. If you would not email the text to an outside vendor, do not paste it.
  • Anonymise where you can. "A mid-market QSR chain in western India" is usually enough context; the client's name and financials rarely add anything to the model's answer.
  • Assume the output is unowned. Copyright and ownership of purely machine-generated text is unsettled in many jurisdictions. Deliverables should carry your analysis and your judgement, not just its prose.

The number to keep in your pocket. MIT's State of AI in Business 2025 found that around 95% of enterprise generative-AI pilots produced no measurable profit-and-loss impact — and attributed it not to weak models but to a learning and integration gap: generic tools that never adapt to the workflow, budgets spent on visible front-office pilots rather than the dull back-office processes where the money actually was. If your recommendation is "buy licences and announce it", you are proposing the thing that failed.

The honest answer is that the change is real, uneven, and smaller at the firm level than at the individual level. Individuals got faster almost immediately. Organisations mostly did not — because the bottleneck was never the typing.

How to interpret it in one sentence: capability is uneven in ways that do not match human intuition, the model gives no signal when it crosses from a peak into a notch, and therefore the only reliable way to know which side a task sits on is to test it — deliberately, on a real example, with a known right answer.

A practical habit: keep a private list. Every time you try a task, write down whether the output was above or below the dashed line for you. Within a month you will have a map that is worth more than any vendor benchmark, because it is drawn on your work.

The same study also named two working styles worth knowing. Centaurs split the work — human takes these tasks, model takes those, with a clear line between. Cyborgs interleave constantly, handing sentences back and forth. Most participants worked as cyborgs; the centaur pattern is easier to govern, because the line is visible to whoever has to review it.

Prompts for the room Q1 · what surprised you?

Capability surprises people report

  • Tone transfer Rewriting the same finding for a CFO, a plant manager and a regulator in seconds, correctly, three different ways.
  • Unstructured to structured Turning 30 messy interview transcripts into a coded theme table — a job that used to eat a junior's fortnight.
  • Reading in your second and third language Gujarati or Marathi customer verbatims usable in an English deck without a translation vendor.
  • Being a sparring partner "Argue the opposite of my recommendation" often surfaces the objection the client will actually raise.
  • The floor rose more than the ceiling In the BCG study the weakest performers gained the most, which compresses the visible difference between a first-year and a third-year.

Limit surprises people report

  • Confidently wrong The tone of a fabricated statistic is identical to the tone of a real one.
  • It argues back Push back on an error and it may defend the error more elaborately rather than concede.
  • Arithmetic It writes a beautiful paragraph around a growth rate it computed incorrectly.
  • No taste for what matters It weights the trivial finding and the killer finding about equally unless you tell it which is which.
  • It cannot be in the room It has no read on the client's politics, no relationship capital, and no stake in the outcome.
  • Time saved ≠ value created Hours freed have to be redeployed into something billable or better, or the saving never reaches the P&L.

Choosing a tool

ChatGPT vs Claude vs Gemini — which, when

"Which one is best?" has no fixed answer, because the three leaders each win a different job and the model line-ups change every few months. The useful question is which one to reach for for this task — and the honest answer, from almost every serious comparison, is that experienced users keep all three open and pick per job. Below is a durable decision framework rather than a leaderboard; treat the specific model names as things to look up on the day.

Which one for this job? click a task
How to actually choose
  • Match the tool to the task, not to a brand. The same person is well served by different tools on the same day — draft in one, research in another, format in a third.
  • Run your own bake-off. Take three or four real tasks you do every week, give the identical prompt to all three, and judge the output you would actually ship. That result beats any benchmark slide.
  • Let the ecosystem break ties. If two are close on quality, the one that already lives inside your email, repo or documents usually wins on friction alone.
  • Re-decide periodically. A new model release can flip the ranking on your task overnight. This is a live choice, not a one-time procurement.
What stays true whichever you pick
  • They all hallucinate. None of the three can be trusted on a fact, figure or citation without a check against a source. The tool changes; that rule does not.
  • Grounding beats brand. Pasting the source material in front of any of them matters more than which one you chose.
  • Confidentiality is your problem, not theirs. What you paste, you have disclosed — use the firm-approved, enterprise-agreement version regardless of vendor.
  • You are still accountable. Whichever logo typed it, your name is on the deliverable.
The one-line rule. Don't marry a tool. Pick the one whose known strength matches the job in front of you, verify the output the same way you would with any of them, and keep the other two a tab away for the next task.

11
Landing

Key takeaways

If you remember only one thing

A large language model is a plausibility engine, not a truth engine. It is at its best transforming material you put in front of it, and at its most dangerous supplying facts from memory in exactly the same confident voice. The work has moved from producing the draft to framing the question and owning the verification.

01

It only guesses the next word

The whole mechanism is scoring every candidate token and picking one, over and over. It is a plausibility engine, not a truth engine — fluency, usefulness and hallucination all come out of that same process.

02

Transformation is safe, recall is risky

It is at its best working on material you put in front of it — summarise, rewrite, restructure, extract, translate. It is at its most dangerous supplying facts from memory. Ground it with the source, or don't ask it.

03

Confidence carries no information — so verification is yours

The prose reads identical whether a fact is solid or invented, and no tool changes that. Check every figure and citation against a source, and remember your name is on the deliverable, whichever model typed it.
