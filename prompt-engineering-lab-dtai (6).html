The CREATE Framework
CREATE is a six-part framework for complex, high-stakes prompts. Character · Request · Examples · Adjustments · Type · Extras. Each component removes ambiguity and gives the model everything it needs to produce expert-grade output.
C · R · E · A · T · E — Every letter explained
C
Character / RoleWho is the AI? Persona, expertise, experience level, and communication style.
R
Request / TaskThe exact action needed. Use a verb + object + scope.
E
Examples / ContextBackground data, reference documents, or example outputs that calibrate the response.
A
Adjustments / ConstraintsGuardrails: word limits, excluded topics, regulatory boundaries, tone restrictions.
T
Type of OutputFormat specification: bullet list, table, memo, JSON, slide outline, email.
E
Extras / EvaluationMeta-instructions: confidence rating, flag assumptions, ask clarifying questions.

The RTF Framework
RTF is the executive's speed formula: Role · Task · Format. Use it for everyday professional tasks where you need a quality output fast.
R · T · F — The 60-second professional prompt
R
RoleAct as a [title] with expertise in [domain]. Sets the knowledge base and voice.
T
TaskDraft / Analyze / Create / Summarize [specific thing] given [context]. Be precise.
F
FormatReturn as [structure] with [sections/length]. Define what you want to read.

✏ Exercise — RTF in 90 Seconds
Meridian's Head of Marketing & Digital Neha Kulkarni needs talking points for a press call about a viral thread alleging a mobile-app outage blocked salary-day withdrawals. Write her RTF prompt.
R: Act as a senior corporate-communications strategist specializing in banking crisis communications and RBI-sensitive disclosures.

T: Prepare talking points for Neha Kulkarni, Meridian Bank's Head of Marketing & Digital, speaking to a journalist about a viral thread claiming the app outage blocked salary-day withdrawals. Bank's position: root cause identified and fixed, no funds lost, affected customers being contacted, branch and ATM channels stayed live.

F: 5 talking points, each 2 sentences. Open with acknowledgement, end with a forward commitment. Avoid speculation on numbers not yet confirmed. Avoid the word "glitch" — use "service disruption."

Zero-Shot Prompting
Zero-shot prompts give no examples — the model relies entirely on its training. They work for well-defined, common tasks. The key is being specific about task, constraints, and format.

Chain-of-Thought Prompting
CoT prompts ask the model to show its reasoning step-by-step before giving a final answer. This dramatically improves accuracy on complex reasoning and strategic decisions.
Sahyadri — CoT in action: exam-hall shortage Higher Ed
P
Prompt (Fatima Sheikh, Controller of Examinations)
Two exam halls at North Campus are unavailable this week after a ceiling leak. 1,900 students are scheduled across those halls for end-semester papers. Think through this situation step by step before recommending an action plan.
1
Step 1 — Assess the hard constraint
1,900 seats lost against a fixed exam calendar. University ordinances forbid rescheduling a paper for one cohort only, so any fix must preserve a common slot and per-candidate seating norms (1 seat/bench).
2
Step 2 — Identify available capacity
Options: (a) two central-block seminar halls (~600 seats), (b) the library reading hall (~300, needs invigilators), (c) South Campus halls (400 seats but 9 km away), (d) a second sitting in the afternoon for the same paper (question-paper security risk).
3
Step 3 — Evaluate constraints
A second sitting risks paper leakage and violates the single-slot ordinance. South Campus transfer needs transport and a fresh invigilation roster. Seminar + library halls together (~900) still leave a 1,000-seat gap.
✓
Recommendation
Immediate: (1) Convert central seminar + library halls (~900 seats). (2) Move the remaining ~1,000 students to South Campus with two shuttle rounds and a borrowed invigilation team. (3) Keep a single morning slot — no second sitting. Escalate to the VC only if invigilator availability falls short.
CoT examples across organizations All Domains
"Think step by step"
The classic CoT trigger. Simple, highly effective.
"Before answering, list your assumptions"
Forces explicit assumption identification — critical for ambiguous situations.
"Walk me through your reasoning"
Produces narrative-style chains — good for executive communication.
"Show your work, then conclude"
Separates analysis from recommendation — ideal when reasoning IS the value.
✏ Exercise — Chain-of-Thought
Meridian Bank's Chief Risk Officer Ritu Aggarwal must decide whether to expand unsecured personal-loan lending by ₹500 crore in a rising-delinquency environment. Write a CoT prompt that makes the model reason through this systematically.
You are a senior bank risk strategist. Before giving any recommendation, think through this decision step by step.

Step 1: Quantify current portfolio stress — 30/90-day delinquency trend, vintage curves on the last 4 quarters.
Step 2: Evaluate what ₹500 crore of new unsecured lending adds in expected loss at current underwriting cut-offs.
Step 3: Examine the do-nothing scenario — NIM pressure, competitive share loss, fixed-cost absorption.
Step 4: Assess portfolio quality levers — score cut-off, bureau triggers, income verification, ticket-size caps.
Step 5: Model capital and provisioning impact against RBI norms and the bank's risk appetite.

Context: Unsecured book ₹4,200 cr, GNPA 2.1% rising, 90+ DPD up 40 bps QoQ, approval rate 46%, blended yield 15.8%.

Only after completing all steps: Expand fully / Expand with tighter cut-offs / Hold + the top 3 reasons + the single assumption that reverses your answer.

Prompt Evaluation — Score It Before You Send It
Nearly every disappointing answer is a briefing failure, not a model failure. This module gives you a way to score a prompt before you send it — on five dimensions — so fixing a weak result stops being guesswork. Paste any prompt into the evaluator and see where it is strong and where it will cost you rounds of rework.
The idea. A good prompt can be judged on five things, each scored 0–4 for a total out of 20. Score before you send, fix the lowest dimension first, and don’t chase a perfect 20 — the last point usually needs data you genuinely don’t have, which is a fact about your engagement, not a flaw in your prompt.
The five dimensions 0–4 each · /20 total
#DimensionThe question it asks
1ClarityCould two different people read this prompt and expect the same output?
2SpecificityAre there real numbers, named roles and concrete details — or vague placeholders?
3Constraint qualityAre the boundaries hard and binary, or soft and advisory?
4Output definitionWould the model know exactly what artefact to produce, and how to structure it?
5Context sufficiencyDoes it have everything it needs to answer without guessing or inventing?

Using this on your own work. (1) Score before you send, not after — catch a missing dimension while it costs seconds.
